Trend

Spill counts have risen sharply since 2021

The number of historical spill records filed each year was relatively flat from 2016 through 2020, then accelerated dramatically. From 2020 to 2025 filings increased more than fifteenfold, from 125 to 1,948 unique spills per year. This reflects a wave of decommissioning activity across Colorado's aging Denver-Julesburg Basin well fleet, as older wells reach the end of their productive lives and operators are required to plug, cap, and remediate them before closure.

Geography

Weld County accounts for 87% of all historical spills

The geographic distribution of historical spills is highly concentrated. Weld County, the heart of Colorado's Denver-Julesburg Basin, has 5,303 of the 6,084 total historical spill records. The next largest county, Adams, has just 187. This reflects both the density of oil and gas development in the Denver-Julesburg Basin and the scale of ongoing abandonment and reclamation activity there.

Operators

Three operators account for 76% of historical spill records

Noble Energy (2,301), Kerr McGee Oil & Gas (1,672), and PDC Energy (665) together account for nearly three-quarters of all historical spill records on file. All three are major Denver-Julesburg Basin operators with large legacy well inventories. Noble Energy was acquired by Chevron in 2020, and Kerr McGee's assets were absorbed into Crestone Peak Resources and then Civitas Resources, meaning the responsibility for many of the historical spills in this report now rests with these companies.

Infrastructure & Land

Tank batteries and wells are the most common failure points

Tank batteries (33%) and wells (30%) together account for nearly two-thirds of historical spill sites, with flowline systems contributing another 19%. Almost all historical spills, 96%, occurred on private land, with federal and state lands representing a small fraction. This has implications for landowner notification and long-term remediation obligations.

Key finding

Why are historical spills being discovered now?

Analysis of the "spill description" and "root cause" fields across all 6,084 records (2016–2025) reveals a striking pattern: nearly every historical spill was found not when it happened, but years or decades later, when someone dug up the ground. Active leak detection during operations accounts for less than 1% of discoveries. The vast majority are uncovered by decommissioning, plugging, or regulatory closure work.

The core finding: Colorado's historical spill record is largely a paper trail of contamination that sat silently underground, sometimes for decades, until decommissioning work forced it into the open. These are not spills being caught and reported in real time. They are legacy contamination events that operators are reporting due to well decommissioning requirements.
  • 41%
    Plugging, abandonment, and cut-and-cap operations When a well is being permanently closed, either through a standard plugging process or under a state-ordered procedure to seal off the wellhead, crews often discover contaminated soil underneath the wellhead, separator, or underground vault. The contamination is usually old, with no clear record of how much spilled, and cleanup is planned as a separate, later project. This is the single largest discovery trigger in the dataset.
  • 22%
    Tank battery deconstruction Workers deconstructing a tank battery find contaminated soil beneath produced water vaults, dump lines, or tanks. The contamination is almost always described as pre-existing with no active leak at the time of discovery.
  • 13%
    Flowline decommissioning When companies remove old pipelines from service, they often find oil-contaminated soil at pipe bends or spots where sensors detect hydrocarbons in the ground. Reports frequently note that an exact release point cannot be determined due to the historical nature of the contamination.
  • 7%
    Facility decommissioning Closure of production facilities, like separators, compressor stations, and other surface equipment, triggers soil assessment that uncovers historical contamination not previously documented.
  • 6%
    Construction and excavation activities Routine construction work, including new pipelines, surface upgrades, and road grading, unexpectedly turns up contaminated soil. These are typically incidental discoveries rather than targeted investigations.
  • 2%
    Site reclamation and pit closure Closing an old production pit or conducting a planned remediation project triggers excavation that exposes legacy contamination not previously on record.
  • <1%
    ECMC field inspection A state field inspector spots pooled fluids or stained soil during a routine site visit and initiates a report. These represent a very small share of discoveries, suggesting inspection capacity is not the primary driver of detection.

Explaining the trend

Why the sharp rise in filings from 2021 onward?

The more-than-fifteenfold increase in historical spill filings between 2020 and 2025 is not primarily a sign that more spills are happening. It reflects several converging forces that are accelerating how quickly and how much legacy contamination gets documented.

  • 1
    Stricter ECMC rules after SB 181 Colorado's landmark 2019 oil and gas reform law directed the ECMC to prioritize public health and environmental protection and led to a comprehensive rewrite of state rules that regulate oil and gas drilling. Enhanced site assessment requirements, stronger criteria for site remediation workplans, and tighter remediation standards now trigger more thorough soil and groundwater sampling when wells are decommissioned, which is leading to more historical releases being identified and reported.
  • 2
    An aging well fleet entering mass decommissioning The DJ Basin saw a major drilling boom in the 2010s. Many of those wells, and the older legacy wells predating the boom, are now reaching the end of their productive life. When an operator plugs and abandons a well or deconstructs a tank battery, ECMC rules, updated after the passage of SB 19-181, require environmental assessment of the site. As the volume of plugging and abandonment activity has climbed sharply since 2021, so has the volume of historical releases being uncovered and documented.
  • 3
    Cut-and-cap program scaling up ECMC's updated "cut and cap" rules require operators to follow a specific set of procedures when plugging decommissioned wells. As this program has ramped up, especially across Weld County's large inventory of older wells, each site assessment completed under the updated rules is an opportunity for industry to find and report soil contamination that has been present for years.
  • 4
    Operator consolidation surfacing inherited liabilities Several large acquisitions in the DJ Basin, notably Chevron's purchase of Noble Energy in 2020 and Civitas Resources' consolidation of Kerr McGee and Crestone legacy assets, brought new scrutiny to inherited well portfolios. Post-acquisition due diligence and accelerated decommissioning programs have pushed large backlogs of legacy contamination into the ECMC reporting system.
What the trend tells us: The rise in historical spill filings is largely good news from a regulatory standpoint. It means the stronger rules related to decommissioning and remediating wells are working. But it also underscores how much historic contamination was never caught in real time, and how much legacy liability remains embedded in Colorado's oil and gas landscape, most of it on private land, and most of it concentrated in Weld County. Finally, the fact that less than 1% of historical spills are caught by state inspectors reveals a need for increased on-the-ground oversight of both active and inactive wells by state regulators.
