Overview

2025 Spills Across the West

Oil and gas operators reported spills across Colorado, Wyoming, and New Mexico throughout 2025, with spill counts falling modestly in all three states but liquid volumes moving in different directions: Colorado's nearly doubled, New Mexico's surged 75%, and Wyoming's fell 21%. A fourth report covers historical spills discovered at Colorado well sites during decommissioning activity, a category that has grown sharply over the past several years. All spill counts and volumes in this report are minimums. Incidents are self-reported by operators to the state, and many reports list an unknown volume. Actual spill activity is likely higher than what the data captures.

Colorado

Colorado recorded 338 spills in 2025, the lowest annual count in the data series since 2016 and down from 378 in 2024. Despite the decline in incidents, total volume spilled nearly doubled, rising from 436,000 gallons in 2024 to 794,000 gallons in 2025. A small number of large spills drove that increase. Weld County in the Denver-Julesburg Basin accounted for the majority of incidents, as it has in every year on record. Looking back over the full data series, incident counts have fluctuated without a clear long-term trend, peaking at 457 in 2022 before declining to the current low. Volume has been more volatile, spiking to 1.46 million gallons in 2022 before falling sharply and then partially rebounding in 2025.

Wyoming

Wyoming reported 786 spills in 2025, down 39 from 825 in 2024. Converse and Campbell counties, both in the Powder River Basin, led the state with 172 and 137 spills respectively. Total volume fell 21%, from 1.81 million gallons in 2024 to 1.43 million gallons in 2025, with the decline driven almost entirely by a reduction in produced water discharge. Produced water remained the dominant material by volume at 1.19 million gallons. Merit Energy reported the highest volume of any operator at 304,000 gallons despite ranking only seventh in spill count, reflecting a pattern of low-frequency, high-volume incidents.

New Mexico

New Mexico saw increases on both measures. Liquid spill incidents rose 9%, from 1,133 in 2024 to 1,237 in 2025, while liquid volume increased 75%, from 2.03 million gallons to 3.56 million gallons. The volume increase is almost entirely explained by a single event: one OXY USA water storage tank failure released 1.66 million gallons of produced water, roughly 39,600 barrels, accounting for nearly half of all liquid volume spilled statewide. Strip that incident out and New Mexico's 2025 liquid volume would have been below 2024 levels. OXY USA led all operators with 1.74 million gallons total. Gas releases, which make up the vast majority of New Mexico's incident count, fell from roughly 40,000 in 2024 to 37,172 in 2025. Lea and Eddy counties in the Permian Basin accounted for 98% of all gas release incidents. Looking back to 2018, liquid spill counts have been relatively stable, averaging about 1,150 per year and ranging from 984 in 2020 to 1,393 in 2022. Gas releases spiked to nearly 52,000 in 2023—likely reflecting expanded reporting requirements—before declining to about 37,000 in 2025, a drop of more than a third in two years. New Mexico's overall incident totals are substantially higher than Colorado's or Wyoming's, reflecting both the scale of Permian Basin production and a reporting threshold that requires operators to report all releases regardless of volume.

Colorado: Historical Spills

Colorado's historical spills report covers 6,084 incidents discovered at well sites during plugging, decommissioning, and construction activity since 2016. These are not new spills; they are legacy contamination detected when aging wells are taken out of service. The count has risen sharply since 2021, driven by a wave of plugging and abandonment activity across the DJ Basin. Noble Energy (now Chevron), Kerr McGee (now Occidental), and PDC Energy (also now Chevron) together account for roughly three-quarters of all historical filings, a reflection of how consolidation has transferred long-dormant liabilities to a handful of large operators. Weld County accounts for 87% of all records. Taken together, these 6,084 discoveries represent an environmental disaster hiding in plain sight—contamination that has likely existed for decades, only coming to light because Colorado requires operators to look for it during decommissioning. Most other oil-producing states have no equivalent requirement, which means the same legacy contamination almost certainly exists elsewhere but goes undetected and unreported.
