New Mexico Spills Report 2025

Source: New Mexico Oil Conservation Division (OCD)  |  Incidents with date January 1 – December 31, 2025

New Mexico was the second-largest oil-producing state in the country in 2025, averaging 2.24 million barrels per day and accounting for 16.5% of total U.S. crude output. The Permian Basin counties of Lea and Eddy drive that production, and they dominate New Mexico's spill record in equal measure.

The Oil Conservation Division received reports of 38,364 oil and gas incidents in 2025, of which 1,230 were liquid spills and 36,694 were gas releases. Liquid spill incidents rose 10% from 1,115 in 2024. Total liquid volume reached 3.51 million gallons, up 73% from 2.02 million gallons the year before. That increase, however, is almost entirely attributable to a single event. One OXY USA water storage tank failure released 1.66 million gallons of produced water, roughly 39,600 barrels, accounting for nearly half of all liquid volume spilled statewide. Strip that incident out and New Mexico's 2025 liquid volume would have been below 2024 levels. OXY USA led all operators for the year with 1.74 million gallons total.

Gas releases fell from roughly 40,000 in 2024 to 36,694 in 2025. Lea and Eddy counties accounted for 98% of all gas release incidents. Lea County alone reported 21,464 releases.

New Mexico's incident totals are far higher than those in Colorado or Wyoming, reflecting two compounding factors. New Mexico is the second-largest oil-producing state in the country, with output that dwarfs both neighboring states, meaning more wells, more pipelines, and more infrastructure that can fail. It also has a reporting threshold unique among the three: operators must report all releases regardless of volume, while Colorado and Wyoming both have minimums below which small releases require no Commission notification. Both factors push New Mexico's counts higher, and neither diminishes what the numbers represent.

Causes

Causes of Liquid Spills, 2025

Equipment failure was the leading cause of liquid spills with 593 incidents (46%), followed by corrosion at 311 (24%). Human error accounted for 83 incidents. Together, equipment failure and corrosion represent nearly three-quarters of all liquid spills.

Causes

Causes of Gas Releases, 2025

High line pressure was the most common cause of gas releases with 9,004 incidents (24%), followed by equipment failure (8,450) and repair and maintenance (5,737). Midstream emergency maintenance accounted for 4,148 releases. Pipeline quality specifications issues totaled 2,647.

Sources of Liquid Spills, 2025

Pipelines were the most common source of liquid spills with 191 incidents, followed by pumps (176) and tanks (128). Flow lines, valves, and other specified equipment each accounted for roughly 100–120 incidents. All remaining sources are combined into "Other."

Policy and Regulation

Rules and regulations

Regulatory oversight. New Mexico's oil and gas spill program is administered by the Oil Conservation Division (OCD) of the Energy, Minerals and Natural Resources Department (EMNRD). The OCD regulates drilling, production, and transportation of oil and gas statewide and oversees environmental remediation when releases occur. Spill reporting is governed by 19.15.29 NMAC, the state's Spill Rule, which was substantially updated in 2018 and amended again in 2021.

What must be reported. New Mexico requires reporting of all releases, regardless of volume, involving oil, produced water, condensate, natural gas, drilling fluid, or any other oilfield waste or chemical. There is no minimum volume threshold. This differs from Colorado and Wyoming, where small or fully contained releases may fall below mandatory reporting thresholds. New Mexico's no-floor approach means the OCD incident database captures minor equipment events alongside significant spills. New Mexico's incident counts are substantially higher than those in Colorado or Wyoming, reflecting both the scale of Permian Basin production and the lower reporting threshold.

Reporting timeline. For major releases, operators must notify the OCD district office verbally or by email within 24 hours of discovery. All releases must be reported in writing via Form C-141 through the OCD's electronic permitting system within 15 days of discovery. Unless a final closure report is submitted within 90 days of discovery, operators must pursue a division-approved remediation plan.

Enforcement. The OCD conducts field inspections and audits to identify violations and can issue Administrative Orders requiring corrective action. Civil penalties can reach $1,000 per day per violation; in severe cases the OCD may suspend or revoke permits. The public can search enforcement actions through the OCD Administrative Order database.

Methodology

Data and methodology

Data source: New Mexico Oil Conservation Division (OCD), downloaded from the OCD permitting and reporting portal as an HTML file (nm_spills.xls, approximately 104 MB). Records cover 2022–2025.

Parsing: The source file is an HTML table export rather than a true spreadsheet. It is parsed using a custom regular expression routine to extract incident records.

Incident classification: Each incident is classified as either a liquid release (produced water, crude oil, condensate, drilling fluid, or other liquid oilfield fluids) or a gas release (natural gas, flared gas, or vented gas) using a classification function applied to the material description field. Incidents with ambiguous or mixed materials are assigned to the dominant type by volume.

Deduplication: Records are deduplicated by Incident Number. Liquid and gas incident sets are deduplicated separately to avoid cross-type collisions.

Volume: All liquid volume figures use the Volume Lost field (total released minus recovered), converted from barrels to gallons by multiplying by 42. A single Incident Number may have multiple rows when multiple materials were released simultaneously; all rows sharing an Incident Number are summed to produce the total volume for that incident. Gas volumes use the Volume Lost field in Mcf, multiplied by 1,000 to convert to cubic feet. Volume Released is not used.

Date parsing: Dates in the source file are formatted as MM/DD/YYYY strings. Year is extracted from the final four characters of the date string.
